Replacing diesel with solar: what your load data should tell you first

Before you compare solar quotes for a factory, hotel or telecom site, measure how and when you use power. Here is what to collect, what it reveals, and how it decides the right system.

Heliora Engineering Team·9 November 2026·4 min read

Many commercial and industrial sites in Nigeria run on two unreliable sources: grid supply that comes and goes, and diesel generators that fill the gaps at a cost that rises with every litre. Solar is now the obvious answer for most of them. The question is which solar system, and how big.

Most businesses start by collecting quotes. That is the wrong first step. Quotes built without measured data are sized on guesses, and a system sized on guesses is either too small to cut the fuel bill or too big to pay back. Start with the data.

What to collect

You need four things, ideally covering at least two to four weeks of normal operation:

  1. Load logging at the main distribution board, at 15-minute intervals or better. This shows the real shape of your demand: base load, daytime peaks, night load and short spikes.
  2. Generator records: run-hours, fuel purchases and, if available, fuel consumption per hour at different loads.
  3. Grid availability: the hours you actually receive grid supply each day, and your tariff band.
  4. A list of large loads: motors, compressors, chillers and pumps, with their ratings and how they start.

What the data tells you

How much of your energy is used in daylight

Solar produces power between roughly 8 am and 5 pm. If most of your consumption falls in that window (a factory on day shifts, an office, a cold store pulling hard in the afternoon), solar can cover a large share of it directly, without batteries. If much of your load runs overnight, as it does for hotels and telecom sites, you need storage, and the economics change.

Your base load and your peaks

The base load is the floor your site never drops below. The peaks decide your inverter rating. Short motor-starting spikes matter too: inverters must handle the surge when a large motor starts, or the system trips at exactly the moment you need it.

How hard your generators are working

This is the number most businesses never look at. Diesel generators are least efficient at light load. Run one below roughly 30% of its rating for long periods and it burns more fuel per kilowatt-hour and suffers carbon build-up, known as wet stacking. Your generator records often show sets that are oversized for the real load and run inefficiently for hours every day.

Three ways to configure the system

Option How it works Best suited to
PV fuel-saver (no batteries) Solar feeds the site alongside the generator or grid, cutting fuel use in daylight Daytime-heavy loads; the lowest capital cost
PV with battery storage Solar charges batteries that carry evening load and ride through outages Mixed day and night loads; sites where outages disrupt operations
Fully off-grid Solar and batteries supply the site, with the generator as rare backup Remote sites, or sites where grid supply is negligible

A fuel-saver system has a limit that is easy to miss. If solar pushes the generator's load too low, it falls into the inefficient zone described above. A proper fuel-saver controller manages this by curtailing solar when needed to keep the generator above its minimum loading. Quotes that ignore it oversell the savings.

Working out the savings

The fuel saving is simple arithmetic once you have the data. Take the energy the solar system will displace each year, multiply it by your generator's fuel use per kilowatt-hour (from your records, at the loads you actually run), then multiply by your diesel price. Compare that against the system's capital cost and its running costs over its life, including any battery replacement.

Do this calculation with your own numbers rather than a supplier's averages. Diesel prices and exchange rates move quickly in Nigeria, so test the result at a few different diesel prices before you commit.

Space and structure

On a rooftop, plan on roughly 5 to 7 square metres of clear, unshaded roof per kilowatt-peak of solar. Before anything is mounted, have the roof checked by a structural engineer. Many industrial roofs were not designed for the extra load, or for the wind forces on a solar array.

Questions to put to any supplier

  • What load data did you size this system on?
  • What share of our annual consumption will solar cover, and how did you calculate it?
  • How does the system protect our generators from running below minimum load?
  • What happens when our largest motor starts?
  • What are the guaranteed performance figures, and what happens if the plant falls short?

A supplier who cannot answer these from your data is selling equipment, not a solution. An independent design, sized on measured demand, gives you a specification you can tender, and quotes you can compare like for like.
