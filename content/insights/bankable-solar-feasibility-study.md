Most solar feasibility studies answer the question "can this work?" A bankable study answers a harder one: "can someone who has never visited the site check that it works?" Lenders, development finance institutions and grant programmes do not take the developer's word for anything. They hand the study to a technical adviser whose job is to find the assumption that does not hold.

A study passes that review when every number can be traced to a source, every assumption is stated, and the risks are named rather than buried. Here is what that takes.

## 1. Demand: who will use the power, and when

The demand assessment is the foundation, and the section most often done badly.

- **For a mini-grid**, that means a site visit and a survey of households, businesses and public facilities: what they use today, what they spend on energy, and what they could afford. Productive users such as mills, cold rooms and welders matter most, because they drive daytime load and revenue.
- **For a C&I site**, it means measured data: load logging at the main distribution board for at least two to four weeks, generator run-hours and fuel records, and grid availability hours.

The output is an hourly load profile, plus a growth assumption that is justified and not simply hoped for. A load profile copied from a template is the fastest way to lose a reviewer's confidence.

## 2. Solar resource

State the irradiance dataset used, such as Solargis, Meteonorm or PVGIS, and the period it covers. Then address local losses that a desk study can miss. In northern Nigeria, harmattan dust can cut output noticeably for weeks at a time, and the soiling assumption should reflect that, along with a realistic cleaning schedule.

## 3. System design and sizing

The study should compare credible options, not justify one already chosen. A typical comparison runs PV with battery storage against a PV-diesel hybrid, with different battery autonomy levels. Tools such as HOMER Pro are standard for optimising hybrid configurations, and PVsyst for detailed energy yield.

Show the reasoning: why this battery capacity, why this inverter rating, what happens on a cloudy week in August.

## 4. Energy yield

For larger assets, lenders expect P50 and P90 yield estimates, meaning the output the plant should exceed half the time and nine years in ten. Every loss assumption should be listed: temperature, soiling, cabling, inverter efficiency, availability and degradation over the project life.

## 5. Costs

- **Capital cost** broken down by line: modules, inverters, batteries, balance of system, distribution network, civil works, logistics, import duties and contingency. A single lump-sum figure tells a reviewer nothing.
- **Operating cost**: routine maintenance, security, insurance, staff, metering and billing, and battery replacement in the years the cycling analysis predicts.

## 6. The financial model

The model turns the engineering into the numbers a funder decides on: levelised cost of energy, tariff, revenue, NPV, IRR and payback, plus debt service cover ratios if the project carries debt.

Two things separate a serious model from a hopeful one:

- **Sensitivity analysis.** Show what happens to returns if demand comes in 20% lower, the tariff is cut, or capital cost overruns. Funders want to know where the project breaks.
- **Currency risk.** Equipment is priced in dollars; revenue is usually earned in naira. A model that ignores exchange-rate movement is incomplete, and reviewers will say so.

If the project relies on a grant or subsidy, model it separately so funders can see the project's economics with and without it.

## 7. Regulatory and permitting pathway

Set out which permits the project needs and from whom. Since the Electricity Act 2023, that can mean NERC or a state electricity regulator, depending on the state. Include land and community agreements, and any tariff approval required. A clear permitting path, with realistic timelines, removes one of the biggest uncertainties a funder faces.

## 8. Environmental and social screening

Funders that apply the IFC Performance Standards will expect an environmental and social screening that identifies risks early: land acquisition, battery disposal, community health and safety, and labour conditions. It also flags whether a full ESIA will be needed under federal requirements.

## 9. Risks and a clear recommendation

End with a risk register (each risk, its likelihood, its impact and the mitigation) and a plain recommendation: proceed, proceed with changes, or stop. A study that never says "stop" about anything is not a study.

## Warning signs of a study that will not pass review

> - One irradiance figure with no source or period given
> - A load profile that was not measured or surveyed
> - Capital cost as a single number
> - No sensitivity analysis, or none on demand
> - No treatment of exchange-rate risk
> - Permits described as "to be obtained" with no pathway or timeline

If your current study has two or more of these, fix them before it reaches a funder. It is far cheaper than a second round of due diligence.
