What an Owner's Engineer actually does on a solar mini-grid project

The EPC contractor builds the plant. The Owner's Engineer checks every stage on your behalf, from design review to final acceptance. Here is what that work looks like, phase by phase.

Heliora Engineering Team·9 November 2026·4 min read

The organisation paying for a solar mini-grid is rarely the one building it. A developer, an agency or a C&I owner signs a contract with an EPC contractor, and the contractor designs, procures and installs the plant. That arrangement works, but it leaves a gap. The contractor is paid to deliver what the contract says, on time and within budget. Nobody on their side is paid to point out where the design is thin or where a crew cut a corner.

An Owner's Engineer (OE) fills that gap. The OE is an independent engineering firm that works for the owner, not the contractor, and checks the project at every stage before the owner signs off and pays.

Why it matters on mini-grids in particular

Most faults on a mini-grid are cheap to fix on paper and expensive to fix after commissioning. A string cable sized for the wrong ambient temperature, an earthing system that never reaches its target resistance, a battery room with no ventilation path, protection settings left at factory default: none of these stop the plant from switching on. They show up months later as nuisance trips, early battery wear or a fire risk, by which point the contractor's crew has left site and the defects period is running out.

Remote sites make this worse. When the nearest spare inverter is a day's drive away, getting it right the first time is the whole game.

What the OE does, phase by phase

1. Before the contract is signed

The most valuable OE work happens before construction starts, because changes on paper cost almost nothing.

  • Design review. The OE checks the single-line diagram, PV array layout, battery and inverter sizing, cable sizing calculations, protection coordination and the distribution network design against the load study and the applicable standards.
  • Technical specifications. If the project is going to tender, the OE reviews or writes the technical specification so that bidders price the same thing.
  • Bid evaluation. The OE compares bids on technical substance: equipment proposed, warranties, guaranteed performance and the realism of the programme, not just the price.

2. Procurement

Once equipment is being ordered, the OE reviews each submittal against the specification: module and inverter datasheets, certifications, battery chemistry and cycle-life data, cable and switchgear schedules, and factory test reports. When equipment arrives, the OE checks quantities, model numbers and serial numbers against what was approved. Substitutions happen. They should happen with the owner's knowledge.

3. Construction

During construction the OE visits site at agreed milestones and inspects the work against the approved design. A typical inspection covers:

  • foundations and mounting structures, including bolt torque and corrosion protection;
  • string wiring, cable routing, labelling and termination quality;
  • earthing and lightning protection;
  • the power house: inverter and battery installation, ventilation, fire safety and access;
  • the distribution network: poles, conductors, clearances, service connections and metering.

Anything that does not meet the design is logged as a non-conformance, with photographs and a required fix. The OE also tracks progress against the programme and checks each payment milestone before the owner releases money, so payment follows completed work rather than invoices.

4. Testing and commissioning

The OE witnesses the tests that prove the plant is safe and performs as designed, and does not rely on the contractor's paperwork alone. Typical tests include insulation resistance, string open-circuit voltage and polarity, earth resistance, inverter and battery functional tests, protection trip tests and a performance check against the expected output for the measured irradiance.

The result is a punch list: the items the contractor must close before the owner accepts the plant. The OE also checks that as-built drawings, test records and operation and maintenance manuals are complete, because the operator will need them long after the contractor has gone.

5. Handover and the defects period

After commissioning, the OE can review the first months of operating data, such as performance ratio, battery state-of-charge behaviour and fault logs, and flag defects for the contractor to fix while the defects liability period is still open. This is also the point to recommend final acceptance, or to hold it back.

What you receive

Phase What the OE checks What you get
Pre-contract Design, specifications, bids Design review report, bid evaluation
Procurement Equipment submittals and deliveries Submittal approvals, delivery inspection records
Construction Workmanship against the approved design Site inspection reports, non-conformance log, payment certificates
Commissioning Safety and performance tests Witnessed test records, punch list
Handover Early operation and defects Final acceptance recommendation

How to scope an OE engagement

Appoint the OE before you sign the EPC contract. An OE brought in at commissioning can only tell you what is wrong. One brought in at design stage can stop it happening.

When you scope the work, agree four things up front:

  1. Independence. The OE should have no commercial relationship with the EPC contractor or the equipment suppliers.
  2. Site visit frequency. Tie visits to construction milestones (foundations, mounting, electrical installation, pre-commissioning) rather than to a fixed calendar.
  3. Authority. Decide whether the OE certifies payment milestones and recommends acceptance, or only reports. Certification gives the role teeth.
  4. Deliverables. List the reports, logs and certificates you expect, so there is no debate later about what the fee covered.

An OE costs a small fraction of the plant's capital cost. On a mini-grid expected to run for twenty years, it is one of the cheapest forms of insurance you can buy.
